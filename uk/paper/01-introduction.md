# 1. Introduction

The National Health Service has committed to becoming "the most AI-enabled health system in the world," with £10 billion allocated to digital transformation by 2028–29 and artificial intelligence identified as one of five transformative technologies in the 10 Year Health Plan for England [1]. Administrative AI has already demonstrated impact at scale: a 30,000-worker trial of Microsoft 365 Copilot across 90 NHS organisations saved staff 43 minutes per day, leading to a full rollout to 505,000 NHS workers [2]. Yet clinical AI — systems that reason over patient data to support diagnostic and therapeutic decisions — lags substantially behind. One-third of NHS Trusts delayed their first clinical AI deployments due to governance delays, complex contracting, outdated IT infrastructure, and staff scepticism [3].

A critical but overlooked barrier to clinical AI deployment is the interface between structured patient data and large language models (LLMs). NHS England mandates Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) R4 for all new APIs [4], and the NHS API catalogue lists 187 production services built on this standard [5]. Any clinical AI system deployed within the NHS must therefore consume FHIR-formatted patient data. However, FHIR's JSON representation was engineered for machine-to-machine interoperability — not for sequential token processing by transformer architectures. A single patient's medication history may consume 2,000–8,000 tokens in raw JSON form, with the majority encoding structural overhead (profile URLs, extension metadata, reference chains) rather than clinically actionable information.

The question of how to transform FHIR data into LLM-consumable representations — the *serialisation problem* — has received growing research attention. Our prior work, FHIRBench [6], demonstrated through a controlled benchmark of 7,200 evaluations that serialisation strategy produces up to 7.5× cost differences and can reverse model quality rankings entirely. Critically, no single "best" format exists: the optimal strategy depends on both the model architecture and the evaluation methodology employed. However, FHIRBench was conducted exclusively on US Core FHIR profiles (US demographics, RxNorm medication coding, US clinical conventions). Whether these findings generalise to UK Core FHIR — with its materially different structures — remains untested.

UK Core FHIR profiles differ from US Core in several clinically significant ways. The NHS Number (a 10-digit verified identifier) replaces fragmented US identification schemes. The Dictionary of Medicines and Devices (dm+d) provides a five-level hierarchical medication coding system fundamentally different from RxNorm's flat structure. SNOMED CT UK edition includes approximately 5,000 additional concepts and British English display terms. NHS-specific extensions (GP practice registration, ethnic category, nominated pharmacy) add structural elements absent from US profiles. These differences may interact with serialisation strategies in ways that alter the cost–quality tradeoffs established in the US context — particularly for medication-related tasks where dm+d's hierarchical structure creates different token profiles than RxNorm.

Concurrent work in the FHIR+LLM space has addressed complementary questions: FHIR-AgentBench [7] evaluates how LLM agents interact with FHIR APIs, and FHIR-Workbench [8] tests LLM understanding of the FHIR standard itself. However, all existing benchmarks use US Core profiles exclusively. No evaluation exists for UK Core FHIR, leaving NHS Trusts without evidence-based guidance on how to optimise the data-to-LLM interface for their specific infrastructure.

This paper presents FHIRBench-UK — a validation and extension of the FHIRBench serialisation benchmark adapted to UK Core FHIR profiles. Using 100 stratified synthetic patients with NHS-realistic demographics and dm+d medication coding, we evaluate the same six serialisation strategies across four frontier LLMs on three clinical tasks. We report whether US-derived recommendations hold in the UK context, identify UK-specific serialisation effects (particularly around dm+d and NHS extensions), and provide the first evidence-based guidance for NHS Trusts deploying clinical AI on their own FHIR infrastructure.
